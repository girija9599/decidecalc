Using a withdrawal projection responsibly

A retirement plan needs a margin for poor market returns, health costs, taxes, changing spending and a longer lifespan. The first-year withdrawal rate is only one starting point. Test lower returns, higher inflation and a longer period rather than relying on one optimistic scenario.

Worked example: the 4% rule tested on ₹2 crore

A ₹2,00,00,000 corpus withdrawn at the classic 4% rate means ₹8,00,000 in year one — about ₹66,700/month. If the portfolio earns 7% while withdrawals rise 5% with inflation, the corpus doesn't just survive 30 years, it grows — because the return exceeds the withdrawal rate. Drop the assumed return to 6% and the picture tightens but still holds. The danger zone starts when the withdrawal rate approaches or exceeds the expected return: at 9–10% withdrawal, even good returns cannot keep up once inflation compounds. Run your own numbers above with the return 2% lower and inflation 1% higher than you believe — if the plan survives that stress test, it carries a real margin of safety.

The 4% rule in the Indian context

The 4% rule came from US market history; Indian retirees should adapt it. Indian inflation (especially healthcare at 10–12%) runs hotter, and market sequence risk differs. Common adaptations: start nearer 3.3–4% (₹50,000–₹67,000/month on ₹2 crore), keep 2–3 years of expenses in SCSS, POMIS or short FDs (the "cash bucket") so market dips never force selling equity at a loss, hold 40–50% in debt instruments, and use the bucket strategy — refilling the cash bucket from equity in good years — as the practical middle ground between rigid rules and panic selling. Rent-free owned housing and family support structures also mean many Indian retirees genuinely need less than the raw formula implies.

Planning tool only: this calculation does not account for taxes, sequence-of-returns risk, annuity income, pensions or asset allocation. A qualified financial professional can help tailor a full retirement plan.

Frequently asked questions

What withdrawal rate should I use?

There is no universal safe rate. It depends on assets, inflation, taxes, time horizon, flexibility and market returns. Model a range of conservative assumptions rather than treating any single percentage as guaranteed.

What is sequence-of-returns risk?

Poor market returns early in retirement can do more damage than the same returns later because withdrawals lock in losses. This simple model uses a steady return and does not capture that volatility.

Should withdrawals rise with inflation?

If spending needs rise with prices, inflation-adjusted withdrawals are a useful scenario to test. Actual spending may vary from year to year.

Is the 4% rule valid for India?

Partly. The US research behind the 4% rule assumes different inflation and market history. For Indian retirees, 3.3–4% is the more commonly recommended starting band, paired with a cash bucket of 2–3 years of expenses in SCSS/POMIS/FDs and the rest split across debt and equity. Treat it as a starting point to stress-test — model lower returns and higher inflation above before trusting it.

What role do SCSS and annuities play in withdrawals?

Senior Citizen Savings Scheme (currently around 8.2%) and Post Office Monthly Income Scheme provide fixed income that covers baseline expenses, letting the market-linked corpus grow untouched during downturns. Annuities (mandatory for 40% of an NPS corpus) add guaranteed income but at lower rates with no inflation adjustment. The standard Indian structure is a blend: fixed income for needs, market corpus for lifestyle and medical inflation.
