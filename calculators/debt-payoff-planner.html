How the payoff methods work

Debt avalanche puts your extra payment toward the highest interest rate and generally costs the least. Debt snowball targets the smallest balance first, creating earlier paid-off accounts that can help some people stay consistent. This planner applies every required payment, rolls a cleared payment into the next target, and assumes no new borrowing.

Worked example: two cards and an extra ₹2,000

Debt 1: ₹1,80,000 at 18% (credit card revolving) with a ₹6,000 minimum. Debt 2: ₹95,000 at 12% (personal loan) with ₹3,500. Add ₹2,000 extra per month. Under avalanche, the extra ₹2,000 hits the 18% card first — clearing the costliest debt sooner and minimising total interest. Under snowball, the ₹95,000 personal loan (with its ₹3,500 minimum) disappears first, freeing ₹3,500 + ₹2,000 to roll into the card. Try both above: the interest difference tells you what the motivational benefit of the snowball is costing you — usually a few thousand rupees, occasionally more.

Debt in India: know your interest rates

The order you attack debt depends on what each debt really costs. Credit-card revolving balances run 36–42% per year — the most expensive money a household ever borrows, and the reason minimum-payments-only can stretch a ₹50,000 balance over a decade. Personal loans cost 10.5–16%, consumer-durable EMIs 16–24% (0% only when genuinely subsidised), and gold loans 9–14%. Before choosing a strategy, also check the balance-transfer lever: moving a ₹1,80,000 card balance to a 12–14% personal loan — or a card offering 0–1% for 3–6 months — often saves more than any ordering strategy, because it changes the rate rather than the sequence. The catch is discipline: a transferred card that starts being used again doubles the problem.

Tips for sticking to the plan

  • Automate the minimums: set NACH/auto-pay for every required payment first — a single missed EMI costs bounce fees, penalties and a CIBIL hit that outlasts the debt.
  • Fix the extra amount in advance: decide the ₹2,000 (or whatever it is) on salary day, not at month-end when it has already been spent.
  • Never pay only the card minimum: card minimums (5–10% of the bill) are designed to keep you revolving at 40%. The calculator above shows what happens when you do more.
  • Negotiate before defaulting: banks convert large card balances into structured EMIs at 14–18% if you ask before missing payments; after default, recovery calls start and options shrink.
  • Keep one buffer: ₹10,000–₹20,000 aside so a medical bill doesn't restart the debt cycle mid-plan.
Important: this is a planning estimate. Keep making at least required payments, check your lender statement for fees or variable-rate changes, and contact the lender early if a payment may be missed.

Frequently asked questions

Is debt avalanche or snowball better?

Avalanche normally produces the lowest interest cost because it attacks the highest rate first. Snowball can be easier to follow because small balances disappear sooner. The best plan is one you can maintain while covering every minimum — run both above and see the actual rupee difference before deciding.

Should I close a credit card after paying it off?

Not automatically. Closing an older card can reduce your credit history length and available credit, raising utilisation on remaining cards and denting your CIBIL score. Consider the annual fee and your spending discipline: if a card tempts you into new debt, closing it may still be worth the small score dip.

Should I consolidate my debts into one personal loan?

Do the arithmetic: consolidating card debt at 40% into a 12–14% personal loan is a genuine win — often ₹60,000+ saved on ₹2 lakh over two years. But consolidation only works if the cleared cards stop being used, and it converts revolving debt into fixed EMIs that still must be paid. Run the combined numbers above against the consolidation offer's EMI.

What happens if I miss payments while following this plan?

A missed payment triggers bounce charges (₹300–₹500), late fees of 2–3% monthly on the overdue amount, and a CIBIL report entry after 30 days that stays for years. If income dips mid-plan, call lenders before missing — most banks offer EMI rescheduling or tenure extensions for borrowers with a clean record, and RBI norms require a proper resolution framework before harsh recovery steps.

How much extra should I pay each month?

Whatever survives your budget after essentials and a small buffer — even ₹1,000/month moves the timeline meaningfully on ₹2–3 lakh of debt. A practical rule: half of every increment (salary hike, bonus, tax refund) goes to debt until the avalanche/snowball is complete. The "Extra monthly payment" input above shows exactly what each amount buys you.

Does paying off debt improve my credit score?

Yes, on balance. Paying down revolving balances cuts credit-utilisation ratio — the biggest score factor after payment history — often lifting CIBIL within 1–2 billing cycles. Closing accounts can trim the score slightly (shorter history, less available credit), which is why the last step of a payoff plan is usually "keep the oldest card, use it lightly".
