How phone EMI is calculated

Whether you finance a ₹12,000 budget Android or a ₹1,00,000 flagship, every phone EMI in India uses the same reducing-balance instalment formula:

Formula: EMI = P × r × (1+r)ⁿ ÷ ((1+r)ⁿ − 1), where P = amount financed (phone price − down payment/exchange value), r = monthly interest rate (annual ÷ 12 ÷ 100), n = number of monthly instalments.

Worked example: ₹25,000 phone at 16% for 9 months

With P = ₹25,000, r = 0.013333 and n = 9, the EMI is ₹2,966 per month — you repay ₹26,696 in total, of which ₹1,696 is interest. On a 9-month no-cost EMI (0%), the same phone costs just ₹2,778 per month with no interest at all. That ₹1,696 gap is real money on a budget phone: it's 7% of the purchase price, which is why cardless no-cost plans from Bajaj Finserv or your bank's festive offer usually beat regular card EMI for mid-range Androids.

Financing a budget or mid-range phone in India

In the ₹8,000–₹30,000 segment — where most Indian phones actually sell — financing comes from three places. Cardless consumer finance (Bajaj Finserv EMI Network Card, HomeCredit, IDFC First) needs only Aadhaar/PAN KYC and dominates offline stores; tenures run 6–24 months, with genuine no-cost windows of 6–10 months but 16–28% interest beyond them. Credit-card EMI works only if you already hold a card, and regular conversion costs 13–24% — worth it only when a no-cost offer applies. Rent-to-own apps (Rentomojo-style or newer phone-rental startups) quote attractive weekly rates but often cost more over 12–18 months than buying outright. One rule matters more than any offer here: a phone loses roughly 40–50% of its value in the first year, so if you're still paying EMIs in month 20 of a 24-month plan, you're paying interest on an asset worth a fraction of its price. Keep the tenure at or under 12 months on budget phones.

Tips before you take a phone EMI

  • Match tenure to resale: budget phones depreciate fastest — 6–9 month plans fit them; 24-month plans suit only flagships you'll keep 3 years.
  • Stack exchange value: an old-phone exchange of ₹4,000–₹10,000 directly shrinks the financed amount — always take it over a pure EMI.
  • Ask what "no-cost" excludes: GST on interest (18%), processing fees up to ₹500, and reduced exchange bonuses can add back ₹500–₹1,500.
  • Check the NACH mandate: cardless finance auto-debits your bank account — keep balance ready on the debit date to avoid ₹300–₹500 bounce fees.
  • Skip financing for sub-₹10,000 phones: if you can't save that in 2 months, the phone is the wrong line item to EMI.

Frequently asked questions

How much EMI will I pay on a ₹20,000 phone?

At 16% interest over 9 months, about ₹2,373/month (₹21,357 total). On a 6-month no-cost EMI it's ₹3,334/month with zero interest, and over 12 months at 16% roughly ₹1,815/month. Set the sliders above to your exact price and tenure.

Can I buy a phone on EMI without a credit card in India?

Yes. Cardless options include the Bajaj Finserv EMI Network Card (available at 1 lakh+ partner stores), HomeCredit, IDFC First Bank's consumer finance, and store tie-ups via Pine Labs or ZestMoney-style platforms. You'll need Aadhaar, PAN and a bank account for auto-debit; approval is usually instant. Interest-free tenures of 6–10 months are common, but regular tenures can carry 16–28% interest.

Is no-cost EMI actually free?

The interest is discounted by the brand or retailer, so you pay only the phone's price — but you may still pay 18% GST on the interest component, a processing fee (₹99–₹500), and lose part of any exchange bonus. Read the final invoice; the effective cost of a "free" plan is usually ₹300–₹1,500, not zero.

What happens if I don't pay my phone EMI?

A missed NACH debit triggers bounce charges (₹300–₹500 from your bank, plus the lender's penalty), then late fees of 1.5–2% per month on the overdue amount. After 30+ days the miss is reported to CIBIL and hurts your score; after 90 days the account is classified as a written-off/NPA stage, which damages loan eligibility for years. Contact the lender before the due date if you'll miss one.

Phone on EMI vs buying outright — which is smarter?

Buy outright if the money isn't your emergency fund. Take EMI only when it's genuinely no-cost (your cash keeps earning while you pay monthly) or when paying upfront would drain your buffer. Financing a ₹15,000 phone at 20% for 12 months adds about ₹1,680 — money better kept in your pocket. See our guide on whether to finance a phone.

Does a phone EMI build credit history?

Yes — consumer-durable loans are reported to CIBIL like any instalment loan. Paying 6–12 EMIs on time can help a thin credit file, but the amounts are small, so the score benefit is modest. The bigger risk is the opposite: one missed EMI on a ₹25,000 phone can dent the score you'll need for a ₹50 lakh home loan later.
