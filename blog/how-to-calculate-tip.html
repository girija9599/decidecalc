How to Calculate a Tip (Fast Mental Math That Always Works)

Nobody should need a calculator to figure out 18% of $86 — but everybody should know the two-second method that gets you there, the pre-tax vs post-tax question restaurants hope you skip, and how to split when three friends only have exact change.

Tipping sits at the intersection of arithmetic and social pressure — which is why payment screens now start at 18% and why people regularly over-tip on bad service or under-tip brilliant servers simply because the math felt awkward in the moment. Fix the math side permanently and the social side gets much easier.

The 10% anchor: the only trick you need

10% = move the decimal one place left. $86.40 → $8.64. Then build everything else from that anchor.
  • 15% = 10% + half of 10% → $8.64 + $4.32 = $12.96 (round to $13)
  • 18% = 20% − a little: 20% is $17.28, subtract ~$1.70 → $15.55 (round to $15.50 or $16)
  • 20% = double the anchor → $8.64 × 2 = $17.28
  • 25% = 20% + half of 10% → $17.28 + $4.32 = $21.60 (round to $22)

Two seconds, no phone, works on any bill. When the amount is ugly, do the math on a rounded bill ($86 instead of $86.40) — the 4-cent difference will never matter.

Pre-tax or post-tax? Why restaurants don't clarify

The service industry convention is tip on the pre-tax subtotal — tax is not a service the staff provided. On an $80 meal with 8.5% sales tax, the difference between the two bases is only $1.36 at a 20% rate, so nobody argues. But on a $400 celebratory dinner with wine, that gap grows to $7+ and the printed "suggested tips" at the bottom of the receipt are almost always calculated on the post-tax total. Knowing the convention lets you choose deliberately instead of by default.

Splitting without the awkwardness

The clean sequence for group bills:

  • Tip first, split second. Compute the tip on the entire bill, add it to get the grand total, then divide. 20% on $210 = $42; total $252; five ways = $50.40 each.
  • Unequal ordering? Each diner adds up their own items, applies the tip rate to that subtotal, then adds their share of tax and shared plates. It takes one extra minute and ends every quiet resentment at the table.
  • Cash-and-card mixes. Whoever pays by card adds the full tip on the card slip; cash contributors hand over their share of food + tip. Never let the card-holder eat the whole tip because "we'll figure it out later."

The modern tip-screen problem

Counter tablets now prompt 18/20/25% for coffee and takeout, and some calculate those percentages on the post-tax, post-fee total. Three defenses: know your number before the screen flips around, remember that custom-tip buttons exist on every terminal, and separate "service was performed" (food to your table, delivery to your door) from "service was transacted" (handing you a bag). Tip the first; the second is optional by every traditional standard.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to calculate a tip in your head?

Find 10% by moving the decimal one place left ($86.40 → $8.64), then scale: 15% = 10% plus half of that ($8.64 + $4.32 = $12.96); 20% = double the 10% figure ($17.28). Round to the nearest dollar — servers prefer a clean $17 or $18 over exact cents.

Should I tip on the pre-tax or post-tax total?

Convention in the US is to tip on the pre-tax subtotal, since tax goes to the government, not the staff. On an $80 meal with 8% tax, tipping 20% on the pre-tax amount is $16.00; tipping on the post-tax total is $17.28 — a modest difference most diners accept either way.

Is 15% still an acceptable tip in the US?

15% remains the floor for adequate sit-down service, though 18–20% has become the displayed default at many restaurants post-2020. Counter service and takeout commonly get 10% or a couple of dollars; delivery drivers typically get 15–20% with a $3–$5 minimum for short distances.

How do I split a tip fairly among a group?

Calculate the tip on the full bill first, then divide by the number of people paying evenly: 20% of a $210 bill is $42, split four ways = $10.50 each. If people ordered very differently, tip proportionally: each person adds 18–20% to their own items plus their share of shared dishes and tax.

Do other countries tip like the US?

No. In Japan tipping can be considered rude; in most of Europe a service charge is included and rounding up 5–10% is generous; in India 5–10% sit-down restaurants is customary where no service charge applies — check the bill first, as many Indian restaurants add a 5–10% service charge automatically.
