How to Calculate Take-Home Pay From Your Salary

A $75,000 salary is not $6,250 a month — after federal tax, FICA, state tax and benefits it is closer to $4,700. Here is the exact subtraction sequence, in the order it happens on a real pay stub, so the number you budget with is the number that actually arrives.

"I make $75,000" is a gross statement. "I spend $4,700 a month" should be a net statement. The distance between those two numbers — and knowing your exact figure before the offer letter, not after the first stub — is the entire foundation of a working budget. Here is the sequence exactly as payroll software applies it.

The subtraction order on a real pay stub

  1. Gross pay — salary ÷ pay periods, or hourly × hours.
  2. Pre-tax deductions — traditional 401(k)/403(b), health/dental/vision premiums, HSA contributions. These shrink taxable income before the IRS touches it.
  3. Federal income tax — brackets applied to taxable income after the standard deduction ($15,000 for single filers in 2026, often adjusted annually).
  4. FICA — 6.2% Social Security (up to the wage cap, ~$176,100 in 2026) + 1.45% Medicare on all earnings, = 7.65% for most workers. This hits gross, not post-401(k) income.
  5. State income tax — 0% in nine states; graduated up to 13.3% in California.
  6. Post-tax deductions — Roth 401(k), union dues, garnishments.

The 401(k) tax discount nobody prices in

Traditional 401(k) contributions dodge federal income tax today, so contributing costs you less take-home than the contribution amount. At a 22% marginal federal bracket, every $1,000 into a pre-tax 401(k) shrinks your paycheck by only about $780. The same logic applies to FSA/HSA dollars and commuter benefits — pre-tax lines are the only discounts the IRS offers automatically.

FICA: the flat toll on every dollar

Federal income tax is progressive — brackets stack — but FICA is a flat 7.65% from the first dollar of wages, with no standard deduction to soften it. That is why effective tax rates are higher than bracket rates for modest incomes: a $40,000 earner may sit "in the 12% bracket" yet lose nearly 20% overall once FICA enters. Above the Social Security wage cap, the 6.2% portion stops — a quiet raise high earners receive mid-year that confuses many pay stubs.

State taxes move the needle more than you think

The exact same $75,000 salary produces roughly $4,540/month take-home in Texas or Florida, about $4,360 in a 4% state like Colorado, and near $4,180 in California (~6% effective here plus SDI). Before relocating "for the salary bump," convert both offers to monthly take-home — a $10,000 raise from Austin to San Jose can be fully consumed by state tax and rent.

Budget on the number that arrives

Every financial rule you use — 50/30/20, rent under 30%, the 20/4/10 car rule — should divide your net income, not your salary. The two minutes it takes to compute take-home pay turn every one of those rules from an aspiration into an arithmetic check you can actually pass.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my monthly take-home pay?

Start with gross salary, subtract pre-tax deductions (401(k), health insurance), then subtract estimated federal income tax (using brackets on taxable income after the standard deduction), FICA (7.65% of gross up to the Social Security cap), and state income tax. Divide the annual result by 12 for monthly take-home.

What percentage of my paycheck goes to taxes?

For most mid-income US workers, 18–28% of gross pay goes to federal income tax, FICA and state tax combined. A $75,000 single filer in a 5% state loses roughly $18,500–$19,500 to taxes — an effective rate near 25% — before any benefit deductions.

Is take-home pay calculated before or after 401(k) contributions?

Both, in sequence: traditional 401(k) contributions come out pre-tax (they lower your taxable income), then taxes are computed on the reduced amount, and the net after everything is your take-home. A 6% 401(k) contribution on $75,000 lowers federal tax by roughly $990 while reducing take-home by only about $3,510 — not the full $4,500.

Why is my first paycheck smaller than I expected?

New jobs often stack deductions at once: a full pay-period of benefits premiums, 401(k) default enrollment, and withholding set from a fresh W-4 that assumes this salary continues all year. Overtime or one-time sign-on payments can also be withheld at the flat 22% supplemental rate, making that check look unusually taxed.

How is take-home pay calculated in India?

Indian take-home = gross CTC minus employer PF contribution, employee PF (12% of basic), professional tax (state-specific, max ~₹2,500/yr), income tax under your chosen regime (old with deductions or new with lower slabs), and any group insurance. A ₹12,00,000 CTC typically nets roughly ₹85,000–₹92,000/month in-hand depending on regime and structure.
